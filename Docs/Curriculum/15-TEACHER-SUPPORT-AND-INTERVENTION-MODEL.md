# Lurexa Teacher Support and Intervention Model

Status: Authoritative human-support model  
Applies to: Lurexa Learn Guided and Intensive modes, teacher dashboards, teacher recommendations

## 1. Purpose

Human teachers are not the delivery engine of the Lurexa curriculum. The asynchronous AI-assisted curriculum must remain capable of moving learners forward independently.

Teacher involvement adds the forms of support where human judgment, social interaction, accountability and nuanced evaluation are especially valuable.

## 2. Teacher role

Teachers primarily:

- interpret learner evidence;
- prioritize intervention;
- facilitate authentic conversation;
- coach speaking and pronunciation;
- review complex productive work;
- motivate and build accountability;
- clarify persistent misconceptions;
- adapt tasks for learner needs;
- validate uncertain performance;
- support transfer to real contexts;
- make high-value professional judgments.

Teachers should not spend limited live time reading slides, repeating app explanations or administering practice the asynchronous system can already handle well.

## 3. Teacher support cycle

Use:

**Review Evidence → Choose Priority → Interact → Diagnose → Coach → Apply → Capture Evidence → Recommend Next Step**

This complements the learner cycle rather than replacing it.

## 4. Scheduled and triggered support

Teacher support may be:

### Scheduled

Regular meetings or reviews, such as weekly Guided sessions.

### Evidence-triggered

The system recommends human attention because learner evidence shows persistent difficulty, uncertainty, imbalance or high-value review need.

### Learner-requested

The learner explicitly asks for help, explanation, coaching or feedback.

### Milestone-based

Human validation is recommended for capstones, important speaking/writing performances, or selected level-exit decisions.

## 5. Intervention priorities

Prioritize teacher time when one or more of the following is present:

1. communication breakdown persists despite support;
2. learner repeatedly succeeds only after heavy scaffolding;
3. productive skill lags significantly behind receptive skill;
4. recurring pronunciation pattern affects intelligibility;
5. learner avoids speaking/writing tasks;
6. learner has plateaued or is disengaging;
7. multiple possible causes make automated intervention uncertain;
8. performance requires social/pragmatic nuance;
9. learner goal is high-stakes professional/academic communication;
10. assessment confidence is too low for a consequential decision.

## 6. Teacher brief

Before a session, Lurexa should summarize authorized evidence into a concise brief rather than overwhelm the teacher with raw analytics.

Recommended fields:

- learner goal;
- current CEFR/module/unit;
- current lesson mission;
- recent competencies practiced;
- strongest evidence;
- unresolved high-priority patterns;
- first-attempt vs scaffolded performance difference;
- relevant pronunciation/interaction observations;
- previous teacher action;
- recommended focus;
- suggested live scenario or artifact;
- evidence to collect during the session.

AI-generated summaries must remain traceable to underlying evidence and should communicate uncertainty.

## 7. Live-session architecture

A normal live session should not mirror the asynchronous lesson sequence.

Recommended pattern:

1. quick retrieval warm-up;
2. targeted conversation/application;
3. diagnostic probing;
4. focused coaching;
5. independent retry/transfer;
6. Create & Apply extension or real-world simulation;
7. concise feedback;
8. teacher observation/evidence capture;
9. next-action recommendation.

## 8. Correction philosophy

Teacher correction follows Lurexa's pedagogical priorities:

- communication-critical → address promptly;
- current target → normally address;
- recurring high-value pattern → address strategically;
- developmental/non-priority → defer when correction would damage fluency or overload attention.

At beginner levels, preserve communication and confidence while correcting salient issues. As proficiency increases, expectations for precision, discourse and self-monitoring increase.

## 9. Teacher override

AI suggestions are recommendations.

Teachers may:

- change focus;
- reject an AI interpretation;
- add context;
- validate or challenge inferred mastery;
- change a live task;
- recommend review or acceleration.

The system should preserve provenance rather than silently replacing AI or teacher observations.

## 10. Post-session loop

After a teacher interaction:

- capture high-value evidence;
- record teacher observation and confidence;
- update recommended review/practice through approved Learner Model pathways;
- schedule relevant asynchronous reinforcement;
- avoid forcing the learner to repeat unrelated content.

The next asynchronous experience should benefit from the teacher interaction.

## 11. Teacher workload design

Lurexa should reduce low-value teacher administration.

Where appropriate, AI may help teachers:

- summarize recent learner evidence;
- propose live scenarios;
- identify likely recurring patterns;
- draft targeted practice;
- compare recent attempts;
- prepare feedback suggestions;
- generate post-session reinforcement.

Teacher remains responsible for consequential judgment.

## 12. Group teaching

Group sessions can be used for:

- conversation;
- peer explanation;
- collaborative problem solving;
- debates/panels at higher levels;
- cultural discussion;
- presentation and feedback;
- mediation tasks.

Group activities should use learner evidence where feasible but must avoid exposing private learner-profile data to peers.

## 13. Expert educator mentorship and escalation

Teachers may receive support from an experienced expert educator or education lead in addition to AI and routine teaching support.

This expert layer is intended for:

- mentoring and coaching teachers;
- difficult learner cases;
- persistent pronunciation or language-transfer questions;
- assessment calibration;
- curriculum and lesson-design review;
- interpretation of complex learner evidence;
- disagreement or uncertainty between AI and teacher judgment;
- professional-development planning;
- modeling strong instructional reasoning;
- advanced teacher-development capstones or leadership work.

Expert educator support should build teacher independence rather than create dependence on one person.

Recommended cycle:

**Case/Goal → Evidence Review → Teacher Reasoning → Expert Dialogue → Joint Decision/Modeling → Teacher Application → Follow-up Evidence → Reflection**

During early Lurexa stages, the founder/education lead may personally provide this support. The product architecture should nevertheless model the capability generically as an Expert Educator/Mentor role so qualified teacher trainers, curriculum specialists and academic leads can be added later.

Recurring insights from expert support should be converted, when appropriate, into scalable Lurexa assets such as teacher micro-courses, case libraries, exemplars, rubrics, AI simulations, coaching protocols, curriculum improvements and better AI guidance.

See `19-EXPERT-EDUCATOR-MENTORSHIP-AND-ESCALATION.md` for the complete model.

## 14. Supported-mode quality standard

Teacher-supported learning is successful when human involvement produces something the asynchronous system alone was less suited to provide: deeper interaction, better diagnosis, stronger motivation, nuanced feedback, higher-quality performance evidence, or meaningful transfer.

Teacher presence is not itself evidence of better pedagogy.

## 15. Definition of success

The teacher layer succeeds when teachers can enter a learner's ongoing journey, understand what matters quickly, provide high-value support, escalate to expert educator mentorship when warranted, and leave the learner with a better asynchronous path afterward.
