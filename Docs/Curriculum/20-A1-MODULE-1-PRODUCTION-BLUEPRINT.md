## 1. Module mission

A1 Module 1 establishes the first complete Lurexa learning loop. The learner should be able to greet someone, introduce themselves, understand basic personal information, ask and answer highly predictable personal questions, spell key information, request repetition/clarification, and complete a short supported introduction exchange.

This module is the reference implementation for all later Lurexa Learn curriculum production.

## 2. Delivery contract

The module must work in all support modes without changing academic standards:

- **Flex:** complete self-paced path with AI assistance and adaptive practice.
- **Guided:** complete Flex path plus targeted teacher review/interaction.
- **Intensive:** complete Flex path plus more frequent teacher interaction and selected high-value performance feedback.
- **Expert educator/mentor:** not a normal learner-facing layer; used to support teachers, calibrate difficult cases, review recurring curriculum issues, and improve institutional practice.

The learner must never be blocked from academic progress solely because a teacher meeting is unavailable.

## 4. Module structure

### Unit 1.1 — Hello and Nice to Meet You

Performance goal: greet someone, respond appropriately, say one's name, ask another person's name, and close a short exchange.

### Unit 1.2 — Where Are You From?

Performance goal: ask and answer where someone is from, identify a small set of countries/nationalities, and use `I am / I'm`, `you are / you're`, `Are you...?` reliably in highly familiar exchanges.

### Unit 1.3 — What Do You Do?

Performance goal: exchange a small set of personal details such as occupation/student status and simple identity information.

### Unit 1.4 — Spell It, Please

Performance goal: spell a name or simple key information, recognize alphabet names, and use repair phrases such as `Can you repeat that?` and `How do you spell that?`.

### Unit 1.5 — My First Real Introduction

Performance goal: integrate Module 1 language in a 45–90 second supported introduction exchange and create a short personal introduction artifact.

# 5. Unit 1.1 — Hello and Nice to Meet You

## Lesson 1.1.1 — Greetings That Work

Estimated async time: 15–20 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can greet someone and respond naturally in a simple exchange.**

Target language:

- Hi / Hello
- Good morning / Good afternoon / Good evening
- How are you?
- I'm good / fine / okay, thanks.
- Nice to meet you.
- Nice to meet you, too.
- Bye / See you.

### Async sequence

1. **Hook:** short visual/audio encounter showing two people meeting.
2. **Meaning first:** learner identifies greeting vs goodbye without grammar explanation.
3. **Listen & choose:** discriminate greeting purpose and time-of-day greeting.
4. **Vocabulary retrieval:** image/situation → retrieve greeting.
5. **Pronunciation:** phrase stress and final consonant clarity in `meet` / `good` where relevant.
6. **Guided dialogue:** choose appropriate response.
7. **Speak:** record 3 short greeting responses.
8. **AI micro-roleplay:** 4–6 turns, greeting and closing.
9. **Review:** retrieve two phrases without visible model.
10. **Reflection:** learner selects which phrase still feels difficult.

### AI role

- stay at A1 language;
- model one phrase only when needed;
- accept semantically appropriate variants;
- do not over-correct accent;
- correct only high-value intelligibility or target phrase errors;
- use learner name only if authorized and appropriate;
- finish with one success + one next target.

### Evidence

Capture separately:

- greeting recognition first attempt;
- response selection;
- spoken production attempt;
- intelligibility of core phrase;
- AI conversation completion;
- amount of scaffolding required.

### Adaptation

If recognition is strong but production weak → reduce MCQ and increase spoken retrieval.

If learner repeatedly confuses greeting/closing → recycle situational visuals.

If pronunciation causes misunderstanding → short Coach-compatible retry, then return to communication.

### Teacher extension

Optional 5–10 minute live warm-up: rapid greeting variation + learner-led opening/closing. Do not reteach phrase meanings.

### Expert educator escalation

Only if recurring cohort data suggests the greeting sequence, pronunciation feedback, or cultural framing is systematically failing. Expert support improves the curriculum/teacher protocol, not one ordinary learner attempt.

## Lesson 1.1.2 — What's Your Name?

Estimated async time: 20 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can say my name and ask another person's name.**

Core:

- I'm ___ / I am ___.
- My name is ___.
- What's your name?
- I'm ___, nice to meet you.

Grammar noticing: subject pronoun `I`, `am`, contraction `I'm`; question chunk treated initially as usable language, not abstract grammar terminology.

### Async sequence

Encounter dialogue → identify names → notice `I'm / My name is` → build chunks → spoken retrieval → guided question/answer → AI roleplay → short writing form → delayed retrieval.

### AI role

Use varied but simple names/cultural identities. Prompt learner to ask the question, not only answer it.

### Evidence

- understands name in slow speech;
- produces name statement;
- asks `What's your name?` intelligibly;
- maintains two-way exchange;
- fills basic name field correctly.

### Teacher extension

Fast rotating introductions or information-gap names task.

# 6. Unit 1.2 — Where Are You From?

## Lesson 1.2.1 — Countries and Identity

Estimated async time: 20–25 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can say where I am from and understand where another person is from.**

Core examples should include Dominican Republic plus a diverse small set of international locations, avoiding a world-culture overload at A1.

Target language:

- I'm from the Dominican Republic.
- I'm from Mexico / Brazil / the United States / Canada / Spain / Japan, etc.
- Where are you from?
- Are you from ___?
- Yes, I am. / No, I'm not.

### Async sequence

Audio/visual profiles → country recognition → vocabulary cards → `I'm from...` retrieval → grammar noticing → listen for country → spoken production → AI exchange → online-style profile line.

### Phonetics

- word stress in selected country/nationality words;
- avoid Spanish-orthography transfer where it affects intelligibility;
- focus only on high-value items encountered by learner.

### AI role

Ask `Where are you from?`, respond to answer, then provide one simple follow-up. Do not introduce complex geography vocabulary.

### Evidence

- listening country identification;
- spoken `I'm from...`;
- question formation as chunk;
- yes/no response;
- online basic profile field.

## Lesson 1.2.2 — I Am / You Are / Are You...?

Estimated async time: 20 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can use `am/are` in very simple identity statements and questions.**

Method: Context → Notice → Meaning → Form → Controlled Practice → Guided Communication → Retrieval.

Do not turn this into a full conjugation-table lesson.

### Error priorities for Spanish speakers

High priority:

- omission of `be`: `I from...`;
- confusion between `is` and other letter/name associations where observed;
- rigid Spanish word order affecting meaning.

Low priority at this stage:

- minor accent features that do not reduce intelligibility.

### AI role

Use mini-corrections after turn, not after every word. Ask learner to repair the whole phrase once.

### Teacher extension

If evidence shows repeated omission of `be`, teacher runs a meaning-focused contrast task rather than grammar lecture.

# 7. Unit 1.3 — What Do You Do?

## Lesson 1.3.1 — Student, Teacher, Worker

Estimated async time: 20 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can say if I am a student and identify a few common occupations.**

Target:

- I'm a student.
- I'm a teacher.
- I'm a designer / driver / nurse / engineer / etc.
- Are you a student?
- Yes, I am. / No, I'm not.

Vocabulary should be useful and expandable; learners may type/say an occupation outside the starter set and receive constrained support rather than being forced into a predefined identity.

### AI role

If learner gives an occupation outside the module set, AI may provide the English term with clear provenance as assistance, then immediately return to learner production.

### Evidence

- understands common occupation words;
- uses article where appropriate;
- produces simple identity statement;
- asks/responds to basic occupation question.

## Lesson 1.3.2 — A Simple Personal Profile

Learner mission: **I can combine my name, country, and student/job information.**

Async sequence: read two micro-profiles → identify details → build own profile → record → AI asks 3 follow-ups → revise written profile.

Create precursor: save profile artifact for Unit 1.5 reuse.

# 8. Unit 1.4 — Spell It, Please

## Lesson 1.4.1 — The Alphabet for Real Communication

Estimated async time: 20–25 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can recognize and say English letter names well enough to spell my name.**

Avoid teaching the alphabet as an isolated chant. Teach it as a communication tool.

### Async sequence

name spelling encounter → letter-name listening → contrast groups → learner name spelling → AI asks for repetition → repair → delayed retrieval.

### Spanish-speaker priorities

Pay particular attention to letter-name confusions that emerge from Spanish transfer. Adapt to observed learner evidence rather than assuming all confusions.

### AI role

When learner spells, confirm what was understood and ask for repair only on uncertain letters.

### Evidence

- letter discrimination;
- spoken spelling;
- repair after misunderstanding;
- first attempt vs corrected attempt.

## Lesson 1.4.2 — Can You Repeat That?

Learner mission: **I can ask for repetition or spelling when I don't understand.**

Target survival phrases:

- Sorry?
- Can you repeat that, please?
- How do you spell that?
- What does ___ mean?
- I don't understand.

### Async sequence

communication breakdown scenario → choose repair phrase → listen and repeat → roleplay → AI intentionally creates one safe misunderstanding → learner repairs → reflection.

### Mastery significance

Repair strategies are essential A1 independence. A learner who knows vocabulary but cannot recover from misunderstanding is not yet functioning independently.

# 9. Unit 1.5 — My First Real Introduction

## Lesson 1.5.1 — Build My Introduction

Estimated async time: 20 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can prepare a short introduction about myself.**

Required content:

- greeting;
- name;
- origin;
- student/job identity where comfortable;
- polite closing.

Optional personal details must never be required if unnecessary for learning.

### AI role

Coach completeness and intelligibility but do not write the final introduction for the learner.

Use graduated support:

independent attempt → small prompt → sentence starter → example → explanation.

### Evidence

Store first independent draft separately from scaffolded final version.

## Lesson 1.5.2 — Introduction Conversation

Estimated async time: 15–20 minutes.

Learner mission: **I can complete a short introduction conversation with another person.**

AI scenario:

> You are meeting another student before a class or activity. Greet them, exchange names and countries, share one basic identity detail, ask at least two questions, and close politely.

Target length: 6–10 turns depending on learner confidence.

AI must:

- remain predictable but not scripted word-for-word;
- allow pauses/productive struggle;
- ask only A1-accessible follow-ups;
- recognize successful repair;
- provide feedback after the short task unless communication breaks down.

### Performance dimensions

- task completion;
- comprehensibility;
- asks questions, not only answers;
- use of core identity language;
- repair strategy if needed;
- pronunciation/intelligibility;
- scaffolding dependence.

## Lesson 1.5.3 — Create & Apply: My Introduction

Artifact options:

- 45–90 second audio/video introduction;
- audio + short written profile;
- accessible text alternative where recording is not possible.

Purpose: introduce oneself to a new class/community.

Audience: simulated or real approved learning community/teacher.

AI support:

- checklist;
- rehearsal;
- pronunciation focus;
- concise feedback;
- no full ghostwriting.

Teacher support:

- optional human response to artifact;
- select one communicative strength and one next priority;
- record high-value observation.

Expert educator support:

- only for teacher calibration, disputed assessment patterns, recurring learner-profile issues, or improvement of feedback protocols.

# 10. Module-level retrieval and adaptation

## Near retrieval

Every lesson retrieves one item from the prior lesson without displaying the full answer first.

## Delayed retrieval

At Unit 1.5, retrieve:

- greeting;
- name question;
- `I'm from...`;
- one `be` question;
- spelling/repair phrase.

## Adaptive branches

### Fast progression

If learner demonstrates reliable independent performance:

- reduce recognition practice;
- skip redundant examples;
- move earlier to speaking/interaction;
- retain required evidence checks.

### Support progression

If learner struggles:

- shorten task;
- increase multimodal examples;
- use one target at a time;
- provide contrastive explanation only when useful;
- add retrieval later instead of endless same-session repetition.

### L1-sensitive support

Dominican-Spanish/Spanish transfer can inform explanations and phonetics, but individual evidence overrides population assumptions.

# 11. Module completion evidence bundle

Minimum evidence bundle:

1. greeting recognition and spoken response;
2. name/origin listening task;
3. independent spoken self-introduction attempt;
4. at least one learner-generated personal question;
5. spelling or repair-strategy performance;
6. short AI introduction conversation;
7. written/basic profile or form;
8. Create & Apply introduction artifact;
9. delayed retrieval after earlier targets;
10. first-attempt vs scaffolded-performance distinction.

Teacher validation is optional for Flex completion unless confidence is too low for a consequential decision.

# 12. Teacher brief for Module 1

If teacher support is available, brief should show:

- completed lessons;
- strongest competencies;
- low-confidence competencies;
- repeated errors/patterns;
- speaking vs recognition gap;
- pronunciation targets affecting intelligibility;
- repair-strategy use;
- amount of AI scaffolding required;
- recommended live scenario;
- evidence to collect.

# 13. Expert educator/mentor trigger

Escalate from teacher to expert educator/mentor when:

- multiple teachers interpret the same competency inconsistently;
- a recurring Dominican-Spanish transfer pattern is not adequately covered by existing guidance;
- teacher needs coaching on correction, pronunciation, assessment, or async design;
- learner case remains ambiguous after normal teacher intervention;
- module data suggests a curriculum-level issue rather than an individual learner issue;
- an assessment/rubric requires calibration.

Expert resolution should be converted into reusable institutional assets whenever possible.

# 14. Accessibility and self-paced continuity

The module must support:

- resume after interruption;
- short session chunks;
- captions/transcripts after appropriate first-listen conditions;
- keyboard-accessible interactions where relevant;
- text alternatives for audio-only production when accessibility requires;
- no penalty for slower calendar pace;
- clear progress based on competencies and tasks, not streak pressure.

# 15. Production acceptance criteria

Module 1 is production-ready only when:

- every lesson can be completed asynchronously;
- AI behaviors are bounded and level-controlled;
- first-attempt evidence is preserved;
- retries/scaffolds do not overwrite evidence;
- speaking is actually required;
- conversation is scenario-based;
- pronunciation feedback is selective and intelligibility-focused;
- teacher support extends rather than duplicates async work;
- expert escalation is available for teacher/curriculum-level needs;
- a learner can stop and resume safely;
- the final evidence bundle supports a defensible A1 Module 1 progression decision.
