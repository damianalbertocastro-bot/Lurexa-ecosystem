# Lurexa Pronunciation & Intelligibility Policy

Status: Initial pedagogical policy — v0.1  
Applies to: Lurexa Mind, Lurexa Coach, Lurexa Learn, teacher recommendations

## 1. Purpose

This policy defines what pronunciation Lurexa should teach, monitor, correct, and remember. The target is effective English communication, not accent erasure.

## 2. Governing principle

> **Pronunciation is pedagogically important when it affects intelligibility, perception, lexical distinction, grammatical audibility, interaction, or communicative effectiveness.**

Difference from one prestige accent is not sufficient reason for correction.

## 3. Pronunciation dimensions

Lurexa may track:

- consonant and vowel contrasts;
- syllable structure and consonant clusters;
- final-consonant clarity;
- grammatical endings when audible in speech;
- word stress;
- sentence stress;
- rhythm;
- reductions;
- linking/connected speech;
- intonation;
- perceptual discrimination;
- intelligibility outcomes.

## 4. Perception before production

Coach must distinguish:

- learner cannot hear/discriminate the target;
- learner can hear it but cannot produce it reliably;
- learner can produce it in isolation but not connected speech;
- learner can produce it when prompted but not spontaneously.

These represent different learning states and require different interventions.

## 5. Beginner orthography rule

For true beginners, written English can activate Spanish grapheme-to-sound decoding before the English sound representation is established.

When useful, teach in this order:

`hear → discriminate → imitate/repeat → connect to meaning → connect to spelling → reuse in speech`

This is a default strategy, not a ban on written input.

## 6. Intelligibility scale

Pronunciation observations use the taxonomy communicative-impact scale:

- `CI0`: accent/variation with no meaningful communication cost;
- `CI1`: noticeable but easily understood;
- `CI2`: listener effort or contextual recovery needed;
- `CI3`: misunderstanding, ambiguity, or task failure.

Actual repair requests or breakdown should be stored as evidence rather than inferred whenever possible.

## 7. Level priorities

### A1
Prioritize sound-to-word mapping, high-frequency lexical intelligibility, core consonant/vowel contrasts, simple clusters, and enough final-consonant clarity to support meaning. Avoid correcting every accent feature.

### A2
Add stable pronunciation of high-frequency morphology and common words, regular-past `-ed` patterns, question/statement intonation where useful, and greater perceptual discrimination.

### B1
Prioritize connected intelligibility, final morphology, word stress, persistent segmental contrasts, and pronunciation that supports sustained conversation.

### B2
Increase precision in stress, rhythm, connected speech, lexical contrasts, professional/social clarity, and persistent fossilized features.

### C1-C2
Target rhetorical delivery, nuanced intonation, register-sensitive prosody, rhythm, stress, high-level listening variation, and persistent features that limit professional or social effectiveness.

## 8. Dominican/Spanish-aware monitoring

Population knowledge may make Coach attentive to:

- Spanish spelling-to-sound transfer;
- initial vowel insertion before English `s + consonant` clusters;
- unfamiliar English vowel contrasts;
- /θ/ and /ð/ perception/production;
- final consonant and morphology audibility;
- word stress/rhythm differences.

None of these may be assigned to an individual without evidence.

## 9. TH rule

For /θ/ and /ð/ difficulty, diagnose perception and production separately. Coach should not repeatedly ask for tongue placement if the learner cannot reliably hear the contrast.

Practice may include:

1. perception contrast;
2. isolated production;
3. word production;
4. phrase production;
5. spontaneous reuse;
6. later revalidation.

## 10. Regular-past -ed rule

A spoken form that sounds like a base verb may represent:

- incorrect past morphology selection;
- correct morphology with weak/absent audible ending;
- connected-speech perception error by the evaluator;
- a combination.

Use written recognition, listening discrimination, isolated repetition, and spontaneous production evidence before assigning a durable cause.

## 11. Grammatical audibility

Pronunciation can interact with grammar. Third-person `-s`, plural `-s`, possessive/final consonants, and past endings should be analyzed jointly when the grammatical marker exists cognitively but is not audible in speech.

Mind may maintain separate states for grammatical knowledge and spoken realization.

## 12. Accent and variation boundary

Do not correct a pronunciation merely because it is Dominican, Caribbean, Spanish-influenced, non-American, or non-British.

Correction requires pedagogical relevance such as:

- misunderstanding risk;
- loss of a lexical contrast;
- repeated listener effort;
- learner's explicit pronunciation goal;
- current curriculum target;
- high-value professional clarity;
- unstable form expected at the learner's level.

## 13. Feedback dosage

In ordinary conversation, pronunciation correction follows the Correction & Feedback Policy. During pronunciation-focused practice, repeated immediate feedback is appropriate for the target feature.

Do not combine multiple unrelated pronunciation corrections in one A1/A2 turn unless communication requires it.

## 14. Evidence states

Useful pronunciation evidence includes:

- perception success/failure;
- isolated production;
- prompted production;
- spontaneous production;
- listener repair required;
- self-correction;
- successful retry;
- later spontaneous success;
- recurrence across contexts.

One automated score must never be treated as unquestionable proof.

## 15. Success criterion

A pronunciation target is improving when the learner increasingly perceives it, produces it with less support, carries it into connected speech, and maintains intelligibility in later spontaneous use.
