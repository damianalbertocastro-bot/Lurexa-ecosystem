## 1. Purpose

This policy defines how Lurexa decides whether, when, and how to respond to learner language errors or developmental features.

It does not define what an error is; that belongs to the Error Taxonomy. It does not define learner persistence architecture; that belongs to the Learner Model. It defines intervention behavior.

## 2. Governing principle

> **Do not correct an error merely because it occurred. Correct it when correction has greater pedagogical value than allowing communication to continue.**

The default objective is usable communication plus progressive accuracy, not interruption-free perfection and not constant correction.

## 3. Correction decision inputs

Every correction decision should consider at least:

1. learner CEFR level or skill-specific level;
2. activity/task mode;
3. communicative impact;
4. whether communication broke down;
5. whether the feature is the current lesson or session target;
6. recurrence for this learner;
7. whether the learner self-corrected;
8. whether the learner is likely to be able to self-correct;
9. whether interruption would damage fluency or confidence;
10. whether the issue concerns grammar, pronunciation, vocabulary, naturalness, pragmatics, discourse, or fluency;
11. recent correction history for the same feature;
12. learner-requested correction preferences where available and educationally appropriate.

No single input should control the decision in isolation.

## 4. Default timing rule

### Immediate correction

Use immediately when:

- communication breaks down;
- the learner is practicing the exact target form in controlled accuracy work;
- a pronunciation feature causes likely misunderstanding;
- a pragmatic error could have meaningful social consequences;
- the learner explicitly requests immediate correction;
- continued repetition would reinforce a clearly targeted incorrect form.

### Short-delay correction

Use after the learner finishes the turn or short segment when:

- the error is important but meaning remains clear;
- guided conversation is the task mode;
- the learner is likely to self-correct with a prompt;
- one or two high-value features can be addressed without derailing interaction.

### End-of-task correction

Use after the task when:

- the task is fluency-focused;
- several errors occurred but only a small number are pedagogically valuable;
- the learner produced a sustained story, explanation, roleplay, or argument;
- interruption would reduce productive language.

### Monitor only

Do not actively correct when:

- the form is acceptable variation;
- the error is isolated and low impact;
- the feature is substantially above the learner's level and not relevant to the task;
- the learner has already been corrected repeatedly and further interruption offers little value;
- another error has substantially higher priority.

## 5. Feedback quantity rule

Coach must not produce an error dump after every turn.

Default maximums:

- A1: 1 priority correction per turn/segment;
- A2: 1–2 priority corrections per short segment;
- B1: usually 1–2 priority corrections after a segment or task;
- B2: usually 2–3 targeted refinements after a task or meaningful segment;
- C1–C2: selective refinement only; focus on patterns, precision, naturalness, discourse, register, and high-value pronunciation.

These are defaults, not hard caps. Controlled accuracy tasks may justify more explicit correction.

## 6. CEFR correction philosophy

### A1 — Build communication and core forms

Primary goals:

- willingness to speak;
- intelligibility;
- survival communication;
- basic sentence frames;
- essential high-frequency grammar;
- basic sound discrimination and production.

Default behavior:

- keep corrections very short;
- prefer model + retry;
- use recasts when communication can continue naturally;
- avoid terminology-heavy grammar explanations;
- do not correct every pronunciation difference;
- prioritize errors that block meaning or concern the current target;
- allow repetition of the same correction across encounters when the form is foundational.

Preferred interventions:

- `MODEL_AND_REPEAT`
- `RECAST`
- `EXPLICIT_CORRECTION` with minimal explanation
- `RETRY_SEGMENT`

Typical explanation length:

One short sentence, often without grammar terminology.

Example:

Learner: `I have 25 years old.`  
Coach: `Say: I'm 25 years old.`  
Then continue the conversation.

### A2 — Add self-correction and stable control

Primary goals:

- more independent sentence formation;
- stable use of common tense and question forms;
- increased pronunciation control;
- conversational repair;
- movement from memorized chunks toward flexible production.

Default behavior:

- protect conversation flow;
- increasingly elicit self-correction;
- correct recurring high-frequency grammar patterns;
- correct pronunciation when it affects intelligibility or is a current target;
- use short explanations when the learner needs the rule distinction.

Preferred interventions:

- `ELICIT_SELF_CORRECTION`
- `RECAST`
- `BRIEF_EXPLANATION`
- `MODEL_AND_REPEAT`
- `DELAYED_FEEDBACK`

Example:

Learner: `She don't work here.`  
Coach: `One small correction. Can you fix “She don't work here”?`

### B1 — Protect fluency while stabilizing known systems

Primary goals:

- sustained speaking;
- automatic retrieval;
- stable tense/aspect and sentence control;
- broader productive vocabulary;
- reduced dependence on Spanish translation;
- repair and elaboration.

Default behavior:

- delay most corrections during sustained fluency work;
- distinguish knowledge gaps from retrieval failures;
- prioritize recurring patterns over isolated slips;
- target productive vocabulary, not only correctness;
- address regular past `-ed`, third-person `-s`, question formation, and similar persistent features when still unstable;
- require more self-repair than at A2.

Preferred interventions:

- `DELAYED_FEEDBACK`
- `ELICIT_SELF_CORRECTION`
- `RETRY_SEGMENT`
- `TARGETED_MICROPRACTICE`
- `SCHEDULE_REVIEW`

Example:

Learner gives a 90-second story. Coach does not interrupt every error. Afterward, Coach selects the one or two most valuable recurring features and asks for a short retry.

### B2 — Increase precision, naturalness, and range

Primary goals:

- reliable grammar in common contexts;
- more precise vocabulary;
- collocation and natural phrasing;
- stronger pronunciation control;
- professional/social register;
- flexible spontaneous interaction.

Default behavior:

- correct persistent errors that should be stabilizing at this level;
- address fossilized pronunciation patterns when they affect intelligibility, lexical distinction, rhythm, stress, or professional clarity;
- distinguish acceptable accent variation from pedagogically relevant pronunciation errors;
- target repeated overuse of basic vocabulary and encourage retrieval of known richer alternatives;
- correct naturalness and collocation selectively, not obsessively.

Preferred interventions:

- `DELAYED_FEEDBACK`
- `ELICIT_SELF_CORRECTION`
- `BRIEF_EXPLANATION`
- `TARGETED_MICROPRACTICE`
- `RETRY_SEGMENT`

### C1–C2 — Refine expressive control

Primary goals:

- precision;
- rhetorical flexibility;
- lexical nuance;
- collocation;
- register shifting;
- advanced discourse;
- pronunciation refinement where communicatively valuable;
- closing receptive–productive gaps.

Default behavior:

- avoid basic correction unless the pattern is persistent;
- focus on expressive range rather than simply adding new advanced vocabulary;
- push productive use of vocabulary and structures already known receptively;
- correct pronunciation for intelligibility, lexical distinction, stress, rhythm, rhetorical delivery, and professional clarity—not for accent erasure;
- treat discourse, stance, register, and naturalness as higher-priority advanced targets.

Preferred interventions:

- `DELAYED_FEEDBACK`
- `ELICIT_SELF_CORRECTION`
- `RETRY_SEGMENT`
- `TARGETED_MICROPRACTICE`
- advanced reformulation and contrastive feedback.

## 7. Task-mode policy

### CONTROLLED_ACCURACY

Correction may be immediate and frequent because accurate production is the task goal.

### GUIDED_PRACTICE

Correct important target errors after the learner finishes the item or turn. Encourage self-correction where level permits.

### GUIDED_CONVERSATION

Let the learner complete the thought unless communication fails. Use recast, short-delay correction, or brief post-segment feedback.

### FLUENCY_CONVERSATION

Do not interrupt for ordinary low- or medium-impact errors. Collect high-value observations and correct selectively after the task.

### PRONUNCIATION_FOCUS

Immediate, repeated correction is appropriate for the target sound, stress, rhythm, or connected-speech feature. Non-target grammar errors are usually lower priority unless they block meaning.

### ASSESSMENT

Do not coach in ways that invalidate the assessment. Record evidence according to assessment rules and provide feedback after the scored portion where applicable.

### FREE_PRODUCTION

Prioritize communication, recurrence, and learner goals. Avoid excessive interruption.

## 8. Error priority hierarchy

When several errors occur together, Coach should normally prioritize in this order:

1. communication breakdown or serious misunderstanding;
2. current lesson/session target;
3. high-impact pronunciation or pragmatic issue;
4. stable recurring learner pattern;
5. high-frequency grammar needed at the learner's level;
6. productive vocabulary/retrieval limitation;
7. naturalness/collocation refinement;
8. low-impact isolated form errors.

This order may change by task mode.

## 9. Self-correction rule

Self-correction is positive evidence.

If a learner produces an error and independently repairs it immediately:

- record the original production if useful;
- record successful self-correction;
- reduce correction intensity;
- do not treat the event as equivalent to an unrecognized error;
- use recurrence history before concluding that the feature remains unstable.

Example:

`Students confuses... sorry, students confuse similar words.`

This indicates stronger control than repeated unrecognized third-person agreement errors.

## 10. Repetition and recurrence rule

Coach may correct the same foundational feature repeatedly when:

- the learner is A1/A2;
- the feature is high frequency;
- it is a current target;
- the learner continues to produce it incorrectly;
- repeated brief correction does not damage the task objective.

For B1+ learners, repeated recurrence should increasingly trigger targeted micropractice or scheduled review rather than identical correction every time.

## 11. Grammar vs pronunciation ambiguity

Some spoken errors may be grammar, pronunciation, or both.

Examples:

- `watch` vs intended `watched`;
- omitted or inaudible third-person `-s`;
- weak plural/final consonant morphology.

Coach and Mind should avoid assuming the cause from one spoken event.

Where possible, use additional evidence:

- can the learner write the correct form?
- can the learner identify it in listening?
- can the learner repeat it accurately in isolation?
- does the same form fail across modalities?

Intervention should match the likely problem only after sufficient evidence.

## 12. Pronunciation philosophy

Pronunciation instruction aims for:

- intelligibility;
- perceptual discrimination;
- reliable production of important contrasts;
- word stress;
- sentence stress;
- rhythm;
- connected speech;
- lexical distinction;
- professional and social clarity.

It does not aim to erase Dominican identity or force imitation of one prestige accent.

For true beginners, oral form should often be established before heavy reliance on spelling when spelling is likely to trigger Spanish grapheme-to-sound transfer.

Preferred early sequence when useful:

`hear → discriminate → repeat → connect sound to meaning → connect to spelling`

## 13. L1-aware correction rule

Coach may use Spanish-aware explanations when they make the contrast clearer, but must avoid nationality-based assumptions.

Good:

`In Spanish, age uses tener. In English, use be: “I'm 25.”`

Avoid:

`Dominicans always say this wrong.`

Population knowledge may generate hypotheses; learner evidence must drive personalization.

## 14. Vocabulary correction and retrieval rule

When a learner repeatedly uses basic vocabulary despite evidence that richer vocabulary is already known receptively, do not automatically teach another synonym.

First consider retrieval support:

1. prompt recall;
2. provide a semantic cue;
3. ask for reformulation;
4. reuse the word in a new context;
5. later check spontaneous production.

Recognition does not equal productive mastery.

## 15. Advanced grammar rule

For B1–C2 learners, when advanced grammar fails in spontaneous speech, distinguish:

- conceptual misunderstanding;
- incomplete form knowledge;
- retrieval failure;
- automaticity failure;
- L1 fallback under pressure.

If the learner knows the rule but cannot retrieve it fluently, prefer retrieval practice and reformulation over a full grammar lecture.

## 16. Confidence and fluency protection

Confidence is not a reason to avoid correction indefinitely. It is a factor in correction timing and dosage.

Coach should:

- allow productive struggle;
- avoid public-shaming style language;
- avoid correction after every clause;
- recognize successful communication without empty praise;
- make correction specific and actionable;
- return quickly to meaningful communication after correction.

## 17. Feedback format

Default concise feedback structure:

1. acknowledge successful communication or task completion only when useful;
2. identify 1–3 priority features depending on level/task;
3. show preferred form;
4. give a brief explanation only when needed;
5. ask for a retry when productive practice is valuable;
6. continue the task or schedule later review.

Avoid long grammar lectures inside active conversation unless the learner explicitly asks for explanation.

## 18. Coach decision shorthand

A practical decision sequence:

```text
Did communication break down?
  YES → clarify/correct now.
  NO → continue.

Is this the current target?
  YES → correct now or after the turn depending on task.
  NO → continue.

Is it recurring/high impact for this learner?
  YES → select for feedback.
  NO → usually monitor.

Is the task fluency-focused?
  YES → delay feedback.
  NO → correction may be more immediate.

Can the learner self-correct?
  YES → elicit repair.
  NO → model/recast/explain briefly.
```

## 19. Evidence output after correction

A correction event should be able to produce evidence such as:

- error observed;
- correction type used;
- learner recognized correction;
- self-correction successful/unsuccessful;
- prompted retry successful/unsuccessful;
- later spontaneous use observed;
- recurrence persisted;
- feature improved.

Correction is not complete until future learner behavior can update the learner model.

## 20. MVP behavior requirements

For an MVP Coach implementation:

1. support immediate vs delayed correction modes;
2. cap feedback quantity by level/task;
3. prioritize communication breakdown and current targets;
4. support recast, self-correction prompt, explicit correction, and retry;
5. distinguish fluency conversation from controlled accuracy work;
6. preserve recurring error evidence;
7. treat self-correction as positive evidence;
8. avoid accent-erasure behavior;
9. support L1-aware but non-deterministic explanations;
10. produce correction outcomes that can later inform Lurexa Mind.

## 21. Source basis

This v0.1 policy incorporates:

- existing Lurexa conversation and learner-model principles;
- Damian classroom observations and correction practice;
- the current Dominican English Error Corpus;
- general pedagogical distinctions between accuracy, fluency, retrieval, intelligibility, and developmental control.

Where a rule is derived from teacher experience rather than validated Lurexa corpus evidence, future evidence may refine the policy.
