# Lurexa English Error Taxonomy

Status: Initial pedagogical taxonomy — v0.1  
Applies to: Lurexa Mind, Lurexa Coach, Lurexa Learn, Lurexa Teach  
Parent authority: `00-LINGUISTIC-INTELLIGENCE-OVERVIEW.md`

## 1. Purpose

This taxonomy provides a stable system for classifying learner language observations before they enter the Dominican English Error Corpus, learner-model interpretations, or Coach intervention logic.

It is designed to answer four different questions without collapsing them into one:

1. **What linguistic feature was observed?**
2. **How much did it affect communication?**
3. **How strong is the evidence that it represents a recurring learner pattern?**
4. **How should Lurexa respond pedagogically?**

The taxonomy classifies observations. It does not automatically prescribe correction.

## 2. Core rule

> **Classify the observable form first. Infer the cause separately. Decide the intervention last.**

An error category must not encode an unverified cause.

Example:

```text
Observed:
"I have 25 years."

Classification:
Grammar > age expression / copular construction

Possible transfer hypothesis:
Spanish "Tengo 25 años."

Learner-specific transfer status:
Unconfirmed until supported by evidence.
```

## 3. Terminology

### 3.1 Observation

A specific learner production, response, comprehension event, repair, or teacher-observed behavior.

### 3.2 Pattern

A linguistic feature that appears repeatedly enough to justify tracking beyond one isolated observation.

### 3.3 Error

A learner form that meaningfully conflicts with the target-language system or intended communicative function for the learning context.

### 3.4 Developmental feature

A form plausibly associated with normal acquisition development. It may still be pedagogically useful to address, but should not automatically be treated as a severe deficiency.

### 3.5 Naturalness issue

A form that is understandable and may be grammatically possible, but is uncommon, awkward, overly literal, non-idiomatic, or poorly matched to context.

### 3.6 Variation

A legitimate or tolerable pronunciation, lexical, grammatical, or pragmatic alternative. Lurexa must not classify ordinary accent variation or acceptable World Englishes variation as an error merely because it differs from one prestige model.

### 3.7 Transfer hypothesis

A proposed relationship between a learner form and prior-language knowledge or communication habits. Transfer is an interpretation, not part of the raw observation.

## 4. Primary domains

Every classified observation should have one primary domain. Secondary domains may be attached where genuinely useful.

### E10 — Cross-Linguistic Transfer

Transfer is primarily an **analytic dimension**, not normally the primary error domain.

Use it to describe a plausible relationship between the observed English form and prior-language knowledge.

Transfer types:

- `LEXICAL_CALQUE`
- `GRAMMATICAL_CALQUE`
- `PHONOLOGICAL_TRANSFER`
- `ORTHOGRAPHIC_TRANSFER`
- `SEMANTIC_TRANSFER`
- `PRAGMATIC_TRANSFER`
- `DISCOURSE_TRANSFER`
- `POSITIVE_TRANSFER`
- `UNKNOWN_TRANSFER_TYPE`

Transfer status must be separately labeled:

- `NOT_EVALUATED`
- `PLAUSIBLE`
- `TEACHER_SUSPECTED`
- `SUPPORTED_BY_RECURRENT_EVIDENCE`
- `RESEARCH_SUPPORTED_POPULATION_PATTERN`
- `INDIVIDUAL_TRANSFER_UNCONFIRMED`

Population research does not establish individual causation.

## 5. Observation type

- `LISTENING_RESPONSE`
- `READING_RESPONSE`
- `PRONUNCIATION_TASK`
- `CONVERSATION_BEHAVIOR`
- `SELF_CORRECTION`
- `TEACHER_OBSERVATION`
- `AI_OBSERVATION`
- `ASSESSMENT_RESPONSE`

## 6. Target relationship

An observation should be labeled according to how it relates to the desired target.

- `INCORRECT` — conflicts clearly with the target system or intended meaning.
- `DEVELOPMENTAL` — acquisition-stage form worth tracking but not over-penalizing.
- `UNDERSTANDABLE_NONSTANDARD` — understandable form that falls outside the preferred instructional target.
- `NATURALNESS` — grammatical/understandable but awkward or uncommon.
- `REGISTER_MISMATCH` — language is poorly matched to the social/professional context.
- `PRAGMATIC_MISMATCH` — intended social action is not performed appropriately.
- `INTELLIGIBILITY_RISK` — pronunciation or form risks misunderstanding.
- `COMMUNICATION_BREAKDOWN` — communication actually failed or required repair.
- `ACCEPTABLE_VARIATION` — legitimate variation; do not treat as an error.
- `UNCERTAIN` — insufficient information for responsible classification.

## 7. Communicative impact

Use a four-level scale.

### CI0 — None

Meaning is fully clear and the difference is mainly form, accent, style, or naturalness.

### CI1 — Low

Meaning remains clear without repair, but the feature is noticeable or pedagogically relevant.

### CI2 — Moderate

Meaning is recoverable but requires listener effort, context, or clarification.

### CI3 — High

The feature causes or is highly likely to cause misunderstanding, ambiguity, or task failure.

Actual breakdown should also be recorded independently when known.

## 8. Correction priority

Correction priority is not identical to communicative impact.

- `P0_MONITOR_ONLY`
- `P1_LOW`
- `P2_MEDIUM`
- `P3_HIGH`
- `P4_IMMEDIATE`

Examples of reasons for higher priority:

- communication breakdown;
- current lesson target;
- high recurrence;
- high intelligibility impact;
- socially consequential pragmatic issue;
- strong fossilization evidence;
- learner explicitly requested correction.

Examples of reasons for lower priority:

- acceptable variation;
- isolated low-impact error;
- feature well above current learner level;
- interruption would damage the current fluency objective;
- another error is more communicatively important.

## 9. Evidence strength

Population-level corpus claims and learner-level interpretations must identify their evidence strength.

### EV0 — Hypothesized

Plausible but not directly observed in Lurexa/teacher evidence.

### EV1 — Teacher Observed

Observed at least once by a trusted teacher source.

### EV2 — Recurrently Teacher Observed

Teacher reports repeated occurrence across teaching experience or multiple encounters.

### EV3 — Multi-Learner Confirmed

Documented across multiple learners or classes with sufficient provenance.

### EV4 — Lurexa Corpus Supported

Supported by accumulated, responsibly collected Lurexa learner evidence.

### EV5 — Strong Empirical Support

Supported by substantial Lurexa evidence and/or strong external linguistic or SLA research.

Evidence strength must not be fabricated when exact data is unavailable.

## 10. Learner recurrence state

For an individual learner, recurrence should be tracked separately from population frequency.

- `R0_SINGLE_OBSERVATION`
- `R1_REPEATED_SAME_SESSION`
- `R2_REPEATED_ACROSS_SESSIONS`
- `R3_STABLE_RECURRING_PATTERN`
- `R4_IMPROVING`
- `R5_NOT_RECENTLY_OBSERVED`
- `R6_REVALIDATION_NEEDED`

A recurring pattern can later become improving or require revalidation. Historical evidence must remain traceable.

## 11. CEFR relevance

Every pattern may specify:

- `minimum_relevant_level`
- `highest_priority_levels`
- `expected_control_by_level`

Do not assume that a form is equally important at every CEFR level.

Illustrative labels:

```text
A1: emerging / heavily scaffolded
A2: developing
B1: increasing independent control
B2: reliable control expected in common contexts
C1-C2: precision, nuance and register refinement
```

Exact expectations should be connected to the English competency model rather than invented independently inside the corpus.

## 14. Pattern ID convention

Each normalized corpus pattern should receive a stable ID.

Format:

```text
DO-ENG-{DOMAIN}-{NUMBER}
```

Where:

- `DO` = Dominican-targeted corpus origin;
- `ENG` = target language English;
- `{DOMAIN}` = primary domain code;
- `{NUMBER}` = zero-padded stable sequence.

Initial domain codes:

- `PRO` — Pronunciation & Phonology
- `GRA` — Grammar & Morphosyntax
- `LEX` — Vocabulary & Lexical Choice
- `NAT` — Collocation & Natural Expression
- `PRA` — Pragmatics & Social Meaning
- `FLU` — Fluency & Automaticity
- `LIS` — Listening & Perception
- `DIS` — Discourse & Conversation Management
- `ORT` — Orthography & Writing

Example:

```text
DO-ENG-GRA-001
```

IDs represent normalized patterns, not individual learner events.

Corpus entries must preserve where the information came from.

Initial provenance categories:

- `DAMIAN_CLASSROOM_OBSERVATION`
- `OTHER_TEACHER_OBSERVATION`
- `LUREXA_LEARNER_EVIDENCE`
- `EXTERNAL_RESEARCH`
- `LINGUISTIC_HYPOTHESIS`
- `SYNTHETIC_EXAMPLE`

Synthetic examples must never be presented as real classroom evidence.

## 17. Dominican corpus distinction

Every pattern in the Dominican English Error Corpus should eventually be categorized as one of:

- `DOMINICAN_SPECIFIC_CANDIDATE`
- `DOMINICAN_SALIENCE_HIGH`
- `GENERAL_SPANISH_TRANSFER`
- `GENERAL_ESL_DEVELOPMENTAL`
- `GENERAL_ENGLISH_LEARNING_PATTERN`
- `UNCERTAIN_OR_MIXED`

This prevents Lurexa from falsely attributing universal ESL patterns to Dominican Spanish.

## 18. Initial annotation workflow

For each classroom observation:

```text
1. Preserve the learner's actual form as closely as possible.
2. Record what the learner intended to communicate.
3. Record the context and approximate learner level when known.
4. Classify the observable linguistic feature.
5. Estimate communicative impact.
6. Record teacher-observed frequency without inventing counts.
7. Add a transfer hypothesis only if plausible.
8. Label the hypothesis as unconfirmed unless evidence supports it.
9. Record what correction the teacher normally uses, if known.
10. Normalize the case into a corpus pattern only when enough context exists.
```

## 19. Guardrails

Lurexa must not:

- treat Dominican identity as proof of a linguistic error;
- treat accent difference as pathology;
- aim for accent erasure;
- mark every deviation from one native-speaker norm as incorrect;
- confuse naturalness with intelligibility;
- infer a persistent learner weakness from one observation;
- fabricate teacher frequency estimates;
- erase original evidence after Mind creates an interpretation;
- overcorrect during fluency work merely because a pattern exists in the corpus.
