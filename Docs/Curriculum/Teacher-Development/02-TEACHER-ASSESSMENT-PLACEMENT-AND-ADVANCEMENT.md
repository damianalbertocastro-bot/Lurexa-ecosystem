# Lurexa Teach Assessment, Placement and Advancement

Status: Authoritative teacher-development assessment specification  
Depends on: `00-LUREXA-TEACH-PROFESSIONAL-DEVELOPMENT-PROGRAM.md`, `01-TEACHER-COMPETENCY-FRAMEWORK.md`

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how Lurexa Teach diagnoses teachers, recommends professional-development pathways, evaluates professional growth and decides when a teacher has demonstrated a higher stage of capability.

The system must never reduce teacher quality to one quiz score, one CEFR score, one classroom observation or one AI judgment.

## 2. Separate diagnostic dimensions

Teacher onboarding should estimate at least two independent dimensions:

### English language profile

Where relevant:

- overall CEFR estimate;
- listening;
- speaking;
- reading;
- writing;
- language-system control;
- pronunciation/intelligibility;
- professional/classroom language.

### Teaching capability profile

Evidence across the nine Lurexa Teach domains:

- Learning and the Learner;
- Curriculum and Learning Design;
- Teaching Language Systems and Skills;
- Classroom Interaction and Facilitation;
- Assessment and Feedback;
- Language Knowledge and Classroom Language;
- Inclusive/Intercultural Practice;
- Digital Pedagogy and Responsible AI;
- Reflection, Ethics and Professional Growth.

## 3. Diagnostic architecture

Recommended teacher diagnostic sequence:

1. background, context and goals;
2. self-assessment with confidence rating;
3. short professional knowledge scenarios;
4. lesson objective/alignment task;
5. learner-error analysis;
6. language-awareness task;
7. assessment/feedback task;
8. digital/AI judgment scenario;
9. short lesson plan or task redesign;
10. microteaching or classroom evidence where feasible;
11. English diagnostic where necessary;
12. synthesis and pathway recommendation.

The system may shorten or expand this sequence adaptively.

## 4. Diagnostic output

A useful output should include:

- estimated Lurexa professional stage;
- confidence in the estimate;
- English-language profile if assessed;
- strongest professional domains;
- priority development domains;
- specific competencies requiring evidence;
- recommended modules/pathways;
- challenge/test-out opportunities;
- recommended mentor or observation support where appropriate.

Example:

```text
Professional stage: T2 — Developing Practitioner
Confidence: Medium
English: B2 overall; speaking B2-, writing B2
Strengths: learner rapport, interaction, lesson delivery
Priorities: assessment design, pronunciation teaching, evidence-based differentiation
Recommended sequence:
1. T2 Assessment for Learning
2. T2 Teaching Pronunciation for Intelligibility
3. Digital pathway: Asynchronous Learning Design
4. Submit microteaching evidence for reassessment
```

## 5. Placement rule

Placement should route teachers to the highest responsible starting point supported by evidence.

Do not force experienced teachers through T1 solely because T1 is the first published course.

If evidence is incomplete, assign a provisional stage and use early authentic tasks to confirm or revise it.

## 6. Challenge and recognition of prior competence

Teachers may challenge modules or competencies where prior competence is likely.

Challenge evidence can include:

- knowledge scenarios;
- lesson plans;
- portfolio artifacts;
- classroom video/audio;
- observation reports;
- assessment designs;
- teaching qualifications;
- prior professional-development evidence;
- interview or mentor validation.

External qualifications may inform routing but should not automatically translate into Lurexa mastery without a clear mapping.

## 7. Evidence quality hierarchy

Evidence strength generally increases from:

1. self-report;
2. selected-response knowledge;
3. constructed-response reasoning;
4. design artifact;
5. simulated professional performance;
6. authentic classroom performance;
7. repeated performance across contexts;
8. impact evidence plus reflective analysis;
9. validated leadership/coaching performance.

This is not a rigid numerical ladder. Evidence relevance to the competency matters more than format prestige.

## 8. Professional stage decisions

A stage is a profile of capability, not a certificate of perfection.

### T1 evidence expectation

Teacher demonstrates foundational planning, instruction, learner support, simple assessment and reflection in familiar contexts.

### T2 evidence expectation

Teacher demonstrates consistent routine practice, adapts based on learner needs, uses formative evidence and teaches core systems/skills with sound sequencing.

### T3 evidence expectation

Teacher demonstrates autonomous unit-level design, diagnosis, integrated assessment, sustained interaction, reasoned adaptation and evidence-based professional judgment.

### T4 evidence expectation

Teacher demonstrates program-level design/improvement, coaching, calibration, analytics interpretation and practitioner inquiry.

### T5 evidence expectation

Teacher demonstrates teacher-development design, training/facilitation, quality leadership, mentoring and professional contribution.

## 9. Advancement rule

Advancement should normally require:

- breadth across required domains;
- sufficient authentic/simulated performance evidence;
- no critical unresolved weaknesses that undermine safe/effective teaching;
- reflective explanation of instructional choices;
- transfer beyond one rehearsed task;
- evidence appropriate to the target stage.

Teachers may have uneven profiles. A teacher can be T3 overall while still carrying T2-level development priorities in one domain.

## 10. Rubric architecture

Performance tasks should use analytic rubrics when appropriate.

Common dimensions:

- goal/competency alignment;
- learner responsiveness;
- pedagogical reasoning;
- sequencing/scaffolding;
- language accuracy/awareness;
- interaction quality;
- assessment/evidence quality;
- feedback quality;
- inclusion/accessibility;
- digital/AI judgment where relevant;
- reflection and improvement.

Rubric dimensions should be selected by task rather than forcing all dimensions into every artifact.

## 11. Microteaching assessment

Microteaching should normally assess:

- objective clarity;
- task setup;
- teacher language;
- learner engagement opportunities;
- scaffolding;
- checking understanding;
- feedback/correction;
- pacing;
- target-language teaching quality;
- evidence of learning;
- post-task reflection.

A polished performance with no evidence of learner thinking should not score highly solely because presentation style is strong.

## 12. Lesson-plan assessment

A plan should be evaluated for:

- communicative/performance objective;
- alignment among objective, activities and assessment;
- progression from support toward independence;
- expected learner actions;
- evidence opportunities;
- likely difficulties;
- adaptation options;
- retrieval/transfer;
- accessibility and context.

## 13. Feedback-quality assessment

Teachers should demonstrate that they can:

- identify the highest-value issue;
- preserve learner confidence without avoiding correction;
- explain the issue at an appropriate level;
- distinguish communication-critical, target-language, developmental and non-priority errors;
- give an actionable next step;
- decide whether feedback should be immediate, delayed or selective.

## 14. Assessment-literacy evidence

Teacher should demonstrate ability to:

- define what a task actually measures;
- distinguish recognition from production;
- create appropriate items/tasks;
- interpret first-attempt versus scaffolded success;
- use rubrics appropriately;
- avoid false precision;
- separate completion from mastery;
- decide what evidence warrants intervention.

## 15. Digital and AI assessment

Teacher should be assessed on judgment, not tool novelty.

Evidence should show ability to:

- identify when AI is useful;
- identify when human judgment is required;
- evaluate an AI-generated lesson/task for alignment and quality;
- detect hallucinated or inappropriate content;
- preserve learner productive work;
- use learner data proportionately;
- protect privacy/provenance;
- constrain AI by curriculum standards.

## 16. Reflection evidence

Strong reflection moves beyond “the lesson went well.”

Teacher should be able to identify:

1. intended outcome;
2. observed evidence;
3. interpretation;
4. alternative explanation;
5. instructional decision;
6. next test or adjustment.

## 17. Revalidation

Professional capabilities can weaken or become outdated. Selected competencies may require revalidation when:

- the teacher has not practiced them for a long period;
- standards or technology materially change;
- later evidence contradicts earlier mastery;
- a teacher changes context substantially;
- calibration is required for high-stakes roles.

Revalidation should be efficient and evidence-driven rather than forcing full course repetition.

## 18. AI role

AI may assist with:

- scoring low-stakes knowledge responses;
- rubric suggestions;
- feedback drafting;
- pattern detection;
- scenario generation;
- lesson-plan analysis;
- evidence summaries;
- next-step recommendations.

AI interpretations must retain confidence/provenance and should not become authoritative professional judgments by default.

## 19. Human validation

Human review becomes more important when evidence is:

- high stakes;
- ambiguous;
- classroom-performance based;
- culturally/contextually nuanced;
- related to leadership/coaching;
- used for employment or institutional decisions.

## 20. Completion versus advancement

Completing modules can earn learning-progress recognition.

Advancing professional stage requires demonstrated performance.

This distinction is non-negotiable.
